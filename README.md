The usual belief is that cache replacement policies such as Least-Recently-Used (LRU) and First-In-First-Out (FIFO) work reasonably well for most access patterns. We show an empirical example in this paper where both policies yield a 0% hit rate precisely on a cyclic memory-access trace, whereas a simple Random-replacement policy achieves more than 60%. We show that this is not a flaw, but rather a well-known pathological case for stack-based replacement algorithms; we reproduce it in isolation using a synthetic trace and quantify it over a sweep of working-set sizes . We then design, implement and iteratively repair an Adaptive replacement policy which monitors a rolling hit-rate window and switches between LRU and Random eviction.  An early version of this policy oscillated so aggressively between modes that it performed worse than even a fixed Random policy (26.9% vs. 61.5% hit rate). We attribute the cause to a symmetric switching threshold and correct it with an asymmetric cooldown policy, recovering to 55.0%, within seven percentage points of pure Random, while matching LRU exactly on traces where LRU is already optimal.
